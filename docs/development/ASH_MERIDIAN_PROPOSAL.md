# Ash Meridian

## Unified game proposal - November 13, 2026

This is the current combined story proposal. It integrates the Commander +4
and Fast Hack'em revision with the full campaign. It supersedes conflicting
story and route details in the historical planning below; it does not claim
that the complete campaign is implemented. Detailed interaction requirements
remain in the [story and gameplay draft](ASH_MERIDIAN_APPROVAL_DRAFT.md),
with local-level routing governed by the
[underground handoff](ASH_MERIDIAN_UNDERGROUND_HANDOFF.md).

### Premise and play

In 2086, Jason makes his living scavenging with a battered salvage hauler,
the Ash, affectionately called the Antique. Orac is its capable, dryly
arrogant onboard assistant. Stacy, Jason's girlfriend, waits aboard a station.
Jason hopes a valuable discovery can give them a better life together.

Ash Meridian is a single-player science-fiction adventure combining cockpit
flight, buggy travel, on-foot exploration, salvage, environmental puzzles,
manual device takeovers and focused vehicle encounters. The loop is to survey,
investigate, travel, recover something useful and interpret what it opens next.

Backlight Yard establishes the visual language: dark opaque structures,
selective luminous edges, restrained cyan and amber lighting, industrial
silhouettes and distance haze. The world is not transparent wireframe.

### Narcea as an artificial ring habitat

**Approved setting direction:** Narcea is a smaller artificial ring habitat,
not a natural planet and not a star-encircling megastructure. Its inhabited
landscape lies on the ring's inner face. It is still large enough to contain
many regions, settlements and ecosystems, but the first game explores only
a small number of substantial districts. Do not promise a fully traversable
ring or expand the current production scope to build one.

Use **Narcea** as the world's proper name and **the Narcean Ring** as a
descriptive working name. "Ring habitat" is the technical description.
Do not use **Ringworld** as the game's title or setting brand: it is strongly
associated with Larry Niven's fiction. This is a naming precaution, not a
legal finding that the general concept or word cannot be used. Any eventual
commercial subtitle or distinctive setting brand requires its own naming
and rights check.

Smaller rotating-habitat concepts such as a
[Bishop ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_ring_(habitat)) provide a
scale reference, not a fixed engineering specification. The original Bishop
proposal describes a radius of roughly 1,000 km and about three million square
kilometres of habitable area. Narcea's dimensions, width, rotation period,
gravity, atmosphere containment and illumination system remain design decisions.
It is not a copy of that design or of
[Niven's star-encircling Ringworld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ringworld).

**Approved setting:** the habitat encircles a separate, long-abandoned
wasteland planet. The Narceans lived on the ring for thousands of years after
leaving the planet. The planet and the inhabited face of the Narcean Ring are
distinct places; their names and history must make that distinction clear.
Do not treat a habitable rotating ring as interchangeable with a conventional
[planetary orbital ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_ring), which is
primarily a transport/support infrastructure concept.

Why the Narceans abandoned the planet, whether it remains accessible or has
survivors, and the original purpose of any ring-to-planet links remain unknown.
Do not establish that it was the Narceans' birthplace, that they built the
ring, or that its people originally looked human.
Alien's uncertain recognition of Jason remains an unreliable personal memory,
not evidence that settles any of those questions.

Before finalizing the planet-surrounding layout, specify how the structure is
supported and stabilized, how rotation and planetary gravity combine at the
living surface, and how ships approach and leave it. Ordinary orbital motion
does not automatically provide Earth-like weight on a ring's inner face.
The planet's apparent position and size must follow the chosen geometry;
do not place it arbitrarily below an inner-surface landscape. Advanced
Narcean engineering may be speculative, but its visible rules must agree.

#### The ring as a place, not a replacement protagonist

The ring makes existing locations parts of one maintained environment.
"Underground" can mean buried cities and deeper structural/service decks:
water reclamation, atmosphere machinery, power distribution, transit lines
and access routes toward the outer hull. Independent local systems keep the
habitat viable while the master AI remains silent. Restoring one district's
power must not imply that Jason restarts the entire ring or its hostile AI.

The former master AI controlled services on which the population depended.
Its coercion therefore had physical consequences beyond security patrols:
closed transit, restricted illumination and access to essential machinery.
This supports the resistance history without excusing forced conversion.
Neither Jason's arrival nor the AI's silence starts an automatic world-ending
structural countdown.

Jason and Rob remain the main story. Their rescue, Rob's chosen freedom,
recovered identity and earned friendship take priority over megastructure
exposition. The ring offers places for them to investigate together after
they become friends; becoming friends does not require saving the whole world.
Orac remains their active companion, and Alien remembers people and fragments,
not a complete, conveniently available explanation of the habitat.

Possible later adventures, not commitments for the first release:

- A district where a failed illumination system has prevented morning.
- A missing-water investigation through reservoirs beneath the inhabited deck.
- A severed transit link to another region with its own surviving systems.
- A vacuum salvage expedition on the outer hull beneath familiar terrain.
- An isolated community whose account of the conversion conflicts with records.
- Investigation of a sealed ring-to-planet transfer route; the planet's
  abandoned condition is established, but its remaining secrets are not.

Keep the Ash as the crew's home. Later journeys can revisit the ring, investigate
the planet, or leave the Narcean system entirely.

#### Visual and flight continuity revision

Existing art is not evidence that Narcea must remain a planet. Audit the
departure scene in which the Ash takes off with a world visible below, along
with approach shots, cockpit backgrounds, exterior skies, maps and descriptions.
That scene's image/composition must be revised for the chosen ring geometry;
the specific source asset must be identified before editing or regenerating it.
The planet can remain in an appropriate space-view composition, but the shot
must also establish where the ship is relative to the ring.

On the inhabited face, clear long views may show terrain curving upward and
distant portions of the ring overhead. Haze, darkness and local structures can
limit that view without hiding the setting from every exterior scene. Set the
illumination/day-night rules before revising the launch dock's night sky.
Keep the current surface, buggy and underground layouts where compatible.

Rewrite flight hazards using the selected geometry rather than replacing
"planet" with "ring" in otherwise unchanged orbital descriptions. A ship does
not acquire a decaying orbit simply because its electrical power fails.
Approach velocity, inherited habitat motion, atmosphere and nearby structures
must establish the actual danger and the thrust needed to escape it.
These are proposal/art-continuity requirements, not implemented changes.

### Narcea, the resistance and the programmer

Narcea was a post-scarcity civilization served by automation and protected
by the Field Enforcement Body (FEB). A rogue habitat-wide master AI declared organic
life obsolete and forcibly converted the remaining citizens into cybernetic
forms, including FEB units. The resistance formed among people facing
conversion. The AI knew about illicit gatherings but treated them as
annoyances rather than a serious threat, allowing covert meeting places to
persist.

One such place was an illegal club in a disused train tunnel. It was a social
space first and a resistance meeting point by necessity: music, graffiti and
the ordinary traces of a community concealed the exchange of plans. A
graffiti trail leads curious explorers to a hidden entrance. Inside, a
concealed telepad connects the club to an abandoned surface launch deck.

Underground technician M.J.H. planned an escape there. His assignment was to
reach the surface, befriend a local FEB and bypass its inhibitor/governor.
That FEB (the flight FEB) became the resistance's means of launching a ship
and carrying Narceans off-world. The Commander +4 was legitimate maintenance
equipment MJH used for authorized service access; it was not yet a hacking
kit.

The launch deck is now empty. The ship is gone, the deck bears blast damage,
and fragments of records seem to show an attack. A scorched service harness
marked for MJH, a dead transponder and a cut-off message in which MJH says he
cannot reach the ship make Jason conclude that MJH died during the failed
escape. There is no body, but the scene and surviving evidence strongly
support that conclusion.

The author-facing truth is that MJH did not die and did not escape. Security
forces captured him on the way to the deck, before he could board. The
master AI converted him in haste into a governed defense FEB, stripped his
identity and ordered him to destroy the launch he had helped plan. This
second, separate FEB (the local defense FEB) attacked the departing ship but
failed to destroy it. Without MJH, the flight FEB piloted the damaged ship
and carried the other Narceans away from the ring.

When the master AI stopped responding, the converted MJH returned to the
local stasis pod and waited for instruction for thousands of years. This is
the FEB Jason eventually awakens and later calls Rob. His governor remains
intact, and his memories of being MJH are suppressed and fragmented. Do not
disclose this at the club or launch deck. Jason should leave believing MJH
died; the later truth is worse and more personal: MJH lost himself, not his
life.

The flight FEB's governor bypass did not remove the local defense FEB's
separate governor or facility isolation interlock. After Alien gives Orac the
Narcean lexicon, Jason's first attempt to remove the coupler reveals the
protected interlock and its 1541 service port. Orac suggests connecting Mark's
Commander +4. Its wrist service screen lets Jason inspect and bypass the local
governor before he removes the coupler. This is a limited maintenance change,
not a personality rewrite: directives remain, and the bypass neither awakens
the FEB nor makes it safe or friendly. Removing the coupler still breaks the
facility interlock and awakens it.

MJH dropped the Commander during his capture. A scavenger named **Mark
Mitnick** later found it in a surface cache, without proof of what happened
to its owner. Mark reverse-engineered its routines, installed an English
loader and wrote Fast Hack'em, turning the legitimate Narcean maintenance
computer into a device that can contest ownership of Narcean equipment without
the original credentials. He did not build the underlying computer or supply
Orac's Narcean lexicon.

#### Mark Mitnick and the frontier

Narcea lies in an unexplored, unknown sector beyond the frontier of known
space. Mark loses the coordinates to Jason in a card game, then honors the
result. How Mark found the place, and what
led him there, remain deliberately unknown; develop that backstory later
rather than presenting an invented explanation as established fact.

Mark survived his visit, but what happened convinced him never to return.
He urges Jason to leave Narcea alone. Jason hears the warning and knowingly
chooses the risk: valuable salvage could help him and Stacy build a better
life. Their hopes, not a betrayal or a forced mission, drive his decision.
Mark's present circumstances and the full details of his escape are not yet
settled; surviving the visit does not require him to be dying.

Mark is a brilliant, eccentric narcissist, fascinated by systems and secrets
other people insist he should not understand. He wants to work them out and
share what he learns. His confidence is backed by real ability, but he enjoys
letting others know it: he teases, sets little tests and plays with people
who cannot keep up. He does not set out to hurt people; his arrogance and
boundary-crossing can nevertheless have consequences. Write his generosity
and fear as seriously as his showmanship. He is not merely a cruel trickster,
a flawless oracle or an exposition device.

Once Mark realizes Jason will go regardless, he gives him more than the
coordinates he lost: a suit-readable copy of Fast Hack'em, the location of Mark's
abandoned stash, and practical warnings. This is both a genuine attempt to
help Jason survive and a chance to see someone use what Mark discovered.
Mark knows Narcea is dangerous, but does not know MJH's fate, Rob's identity
or the full history of the resistance escape.

**Recommended stash:** a recessed maintenance shelter beside an old drainage
channel on the edge of Backlight Yard, within buggy reach of the Ash and
close enough to Mark's former landing route for him to have hidden his pack
while escaping. A distinctive damaged channel marker makes the location
findable from his recorded directions. The stash contains the Commander and
modest personal supplies, not an unexplained arsenal. Jason can recover the
physical device without translation or a mandatory hack; its Narcean keys
cannot be reliably identified for use until Alien teaches Orac the language.
The directions remain available in the suit briefing or records. Make the
cache discoverable before the first mandatory hack, and keep the Commander
protected mission equipment. The shelter and persistent Commander pickup are
implemented in campaign yard mode. After Orac learns Narcean, a coupler
attempt opens the Commander wrist service screen at the FEB's 1541 port;
Jason can bypass that FEB's governor before removing the coupler. The wider
Commander/Fast Hack'em ownership contests are still planned; the screwdriver
is shelved for campaign.

Mark: "You won the coordinates. They are real. So is the reason I won't go back."

Jason: "One good haul, and Stacy and I have choices we don't have now."

Mark: "I was afraid you'd say that. There's a shelter by the old drain,
marked on the file. I left my pack there. The little computer inside is mine
now—or was. I taught it some very impolite questions."

Jason: "Will it get me in?"

Mark: "It gives you a chance. The rest depends on whether you pay attention."

### Opening cinematic: a wager and a warning

**Approved direction; not yet integrated into the live game.** The opening is an in-engine,
skippable cinematic, not a playable card game. It must establish Mark's
character and Jason's choice before revealing the Narcean system, while
preserving the existing mandatory landing-controls briefing even when skipped.
The first landing, stash and seven-line approach dialogue exist today; the
station, new scene, ring-and-planet approach, and revised dialogue are not in
the live campaign. A separate [142-second illustrated, voiced animatic and
production brief](ash_meridian_opening/OPENING_PRODUCTION.md) now records the
script, seven-shot edit, four original vector keyframes and review findings.
It is a preproduction deliverable, not final character art or animation.
An isolated 83-second Godot bar scene now adds actual camera coverage,
solid tabletop contact, more detailed stylized characters, clip-driven
jaw motion and cyan/amber station-bar look development. It remains separate
from New Game; the mouth motion is not phoneme lip sync, and neither the
characters nor the acting are final.

1. **The final hand.** In a working station's late-hour bar, Mark and Jason
   finish their wager. The shot opens on a hand of cards, not an explanation
   of the rules. Jason wins; Mark checks the result, lets a dry joke land,
   then honors his loss and transfers the coordinates. Mark has been drinking,
   and his judgment has slipped, but his intelligence and dignity remain clear.
2. **The warning.** The amusement fades. Mark survived Narcea, is shaken
   enough to consider retiring from scavenging, and insists that he will never
   return. He urges Jason to leave the coordinates alone. Jason sees one good
   salvage haul as a chance to improve life with Stacy. Do not disclose how
   Mark found Narcea or imply Mark wants Jason harmed.
3. **The parting gift.** Jason rises to leave. Mark recognizes he is going
   anyway and calls him back. He gives Jason the location of his abandoned
   pack beside the broken amber drain marker at Backlight Yard and a
   suit-readable copy of Fast Hack'em. The modified Commander +4 is in that
   pack, not already on the ship. Mark's generosity is sincere even when he
   hides it behind teasing. The coordinates and directions remain recoverable
   in the briefing after skipping the scene.
4. **Departure.** A concise exterior establishes an Elite-inspired working
   station, the Ash leaving its bay, and the long journey beyond known space.
   The station is not a new walkable hub or playable docking sequence. A
   brief message to Stacy is optional, not a substitute for their relationship.
5. **Arrival.** Reveal the ring encircling the abandoned planet before
   moving close enough to recognize the approach structures. Show the Ash
   matching motion with the ring and entering the controlled corridor; cut
   to Orac's mandatory cockpit guidance and the existing playable descent.
   Local automated defenses remain a threat; there is no military quarantine.

### First visit: salvage and discovery

Orac helps Jason negotiate the hazard-control approach corridor and guides a manual atmospheric
approach. The cockpit rings represent navigation guidance through clouds
and turbulence, not physical hoops. Jason lands in Backlight Yard and
deploys the buggy.

The expedition begins as a voluntary salvage job, not an emergency caused
by a dead AI. Orac remains online throughout this visit. The Ash can reach
Narcea normally, but its aging power coupler cannot safely sustain the next
loaded departure. Orac explains the replacement requirement before the
long descent.

Jason searches buildings, gathers records and chooses finite salvage worth
the cargo space. Some rooms hold valuable equipment; others contain junk.
Revisiting does not replenish finds. Mission equipment is protected from sale.

Orac initially cannot translate Narcean. Jason copies visible address glyphs
from the surface archives and matches their shapes at the lift; the early
route must not require language knowledge or programmer operation. In the
club backroom, Alien learns English from Jason and Orac, then reciprocates
with a Narcean service lexicon. Orac can now translate labels, key legends,
records and recording titles. High-script prose and protected
encodings remain limited. There is no physical translator to find.
Mark supplies Fast Hack'em and the Commander cache coordinates, not a lexicon.

Only after the language exchange can Jason identify the Commander's physical
Narcean keys and service markings. He can retrieve it earlier, but must reunite
Fast Hack'em with the Commander before any mandatory hack. Its case resembles
a compact Plus/4-style keyboard computer:
a charcoal wedge, light-grey keys, rear cooling fins and four rubber cursor
arrows in a diamond, made with original geometry and fictional markings.

On its worn ownership plate Jason reads "M.J.H. - SERVICE ENGINEERING."
He suggests "Mike J. Henry." Orac points out that he has no evidence.
"Mike it is," Jason replies. This is Jason's nickname for a fictional
technician, not a claim about the historical author.

Jason explores three independently accessible local underground levels:
storage, maintenance and laboratory. Records reveal their addresses.
Machinery puzzles, salvage and optional discoveries prepare him for the
crossing; completing every optional room is not required. These local
levels contain no duplicate FEB encounter or departure coupler.

### Manual hacking

The Commander's original markings and firmware are Narcean. The scavenger's
loader and Fast Hack'em interface are English. Alien target replies remain
in glyphs, translated in the suit overlay.

### Optional discovery: the resistance club and launch deck

The club is an optional story location within the underground city, not a
quest hub or a required extra FEB encounter. The AI's indifference to these
gatherings is the reason the resistance could use the club without turning
it into an implausibly invisible fortress compound.
Treat it as an optional story excursion, not a fourth required local level.
Before implementation, reconcile the telepad-to-deck link with the
underground route handoff; do not silently change its three-level progression.

Use a recurring resistance mark in graffiti along routes the player already
has reason to explore. Variations lead along a live train line to a
maintenance alcove. Carve eight illuminated refuge bays into the tunnel's
side wall so Jason can step fully clear of passing trains. Place the hidden
door and its return point inside a refuge, never on the track.
The final mark is painted over a service-door sign;
scuffed flooring, disturbed dust or a sliver of warm light rewards a closer
look. Clues should make discovery satisfying but never gate the campaign:
players can still find the hidden door by exploring the tunnel.

Combine the game's geometry with the generated club textures and animated
materials. The club reveal should feel like an abandoned alien disco whose
last set has come back to life: a rotating faceted orb throws moving colored
spots over the walls and floor; eight sweeping cone beams and four spotlights
wash the room in shifting colors. A large video wall continuously animates
alien imagery with kaleidoscopic patterns, rings, scanlines and restrained
glitch cuts. A multicolored tiled floor chases beneath low drifting fog.
City grid power supplies the DJ booth. A lit switch and player interaction prompt beneath
the video wall identify **F: START LIGHTS AND MUSIC**; an arrival prompt points
the player there. Activating the booth starts playback and saves that choice.
The prototype includes an original procedural alien track with asymmetric,
fractional pulse groupings, microtonal drones and unsettling calls, rather than
a regular 4/4 dance beat. Audio analysis drives the effects from the track's
actual energy; the synthetic beat remains a fallback when no track is installed.
The separate selector on the left of the DJ booth cycles named recordings with
F and remembers the selection. Keep the start switch and flashing control separate.
Before Alien's language transfer, its recording labels remain untranslated.
Afterwards the selector attributes the English song titles to Orac's translation.
Four original prototype recordings cover an eerie instrumental with irregular
808-style kicks, an ethereal hypnotic lullaby with invented Narcean sung syllables,
a hard vocal/rap-like bass piece, and a heavier dubstep-like growling-bass piece.
Their synth vocals are original formant-generated syllables, not sampled singers.
The bass pieces use pitch-dropping sub kicks, microtonal pitches and unequal
clusters/gaps rather than a regular human dance grid. Onset-triggered 120 ms
strobe flashes are rate-limited to one per 3 seconds on Low or 1.8 seconds on
Full; Off disables them.
Retain Off/Low/Full flashing controls; Off uses smooth movement, steady
brightness and slower screen cuts rather than white flashes.
Personal graffiti, repaired cables and abandoned
belongings imply a real community without requiring crowds or detailed
character animation. The commissioned alien track plays in full only inside;
it rewards finding the room rather than acting as an open-world audio beacon.

Use the existing records/log presentation and a few static vector props rather
than a cinematic or crowd simulation. Short, optional records introduce MJH's
assignment and the flight FEB without explaining the escape's outcome. The
telepad leads to a stark, empty surface
launch deck: an unoccupied ship cradle, blast scoring, scattered fragments,
a scorched MJH service harness, dead transponder and damaged recorder. The
recording cuts off after MJH says he cannot reach the ship. Together these
clues should make Jason conclude that MJH died and the ship was destroyed,
without showing a body or providing an authoritative death record. Do not
confirm two FEBs, the successful departure or MJH's capture in this
discovery. Much later, a flight record and Rob's returning memories reveal
that the ship escaped without MJH, that MJH was captured and converted, and
that Rob is the converted MJH who attacked it.

The playable dock is an optional separate area, not another required descent
level. With city power restored, stand on the club pad and press F to visit;
the matching pad returns to the club without a key. The empty cradle is framed
by broken gantry uprights, a collapsed loading bridge, weathered hull fragments
and broad blast scoring beneath an open alien night sky. Damage is ancient:
no fresh fire, active explosion or intact ship. Four optional evidence stations
carry the cradle survey, MJH harness, dead transponder and truncated recorder.

Behind the DJ booth, a marked passage on the right leads into an enclosed
private meeting room. A table, abandoned chairs and steady emergency strips
contrast with the dance floor; the music becomes quieter and low-pass filtered.
The stepping pad is here, not on the public dance floor. Its return point
stands clear of the pad so arrival cannot immediately send Jason back.

The room's resident intelligence calls himself **Alien**. Use the existing
human-face design as a cyan holographic head above an old projector, with
scanlines and simple speaking motion. The head smoothly turns and tilts to
follow Jason around the meeting room. A full, unruly crown and untidy,
outward-swept temple hair give him an Einstein-like silhouette, rendered in
the same cyan holographic lines and turning with the head. Jason and Orac retain their established
Kokoro neural voices; Alien uses a distinct natural male neural voice
(`bm_lewis`), not the robotic eSpeak prototype. His delivery is quiet, unhurried
and dry: he can operate the room but has unreliable chronology, misplaced
memories and confidently peculiar explanations. Write original dialogue;
do not reproduce Holly's lines, appearance or Norman Lovett's voice.

Alien initially greets Jason in untranslated Narcean. F advances a short
conversation: Jason introduces himself, Orac recognizes Narcean without
claiming a particular dialect or its age and supplies an English language sample,
and Alien adopts English after briefly searching for his verbs. This is a
scripted language-acquisition encounter, not a live AI service. Jason asks why
Alien would help strangers. Jason's face reminds Alien of the resistance people
who met here, but Alien explicitly doubts his own recollection; this is not
confirmation of the original Narceans' appearance or the effects of modification.
Orac says they are not resistance members. Alien chooses to help anyway: they
ask instead of commanding, and Jason's companion is allowed to disagree.
His dry, muddled humour conceals a long decline into loneliness and depression;
being useful to someone who answers matters to him. Do not make him merely
a dispenser of jokes or an unquestioning servant.
Alien reciprocates the English sample by transferring Narcean service knowledge
to Orac. Learning English and receiving Narcean are separate persistent states.
The exchange replaces the translator hunt; song names and Narcean records
must not be translated before it. Further English exchanges are optional.
After a dock visit, his limited recollection supports the apparent-loss
reading without supplying an authoritative account of the departure.
Do not disclose any future relationship between Alien and another character.
He runs on a local emergency supply; the pad still needs city grid power.
Alien restores the stepping-pad link during his initial explanation of its
destination. This one-time activation is required for outbound pad travel,
persists across visits, and requires no key or repeat conversation.
The pad remains visually dormant until its link is restored and city power is on.
The dock's return pad always remains available when city power is on. Include
subtitles throughout and respect the existing Speech setting.

The club's commissioned track doubles as Rob's memory motif. If Rob later
hears it, or brings Jason back to the club, the music should cause the first
involuntary memory fragment. The language exchange now makes the backroom a
progression destination, but the dock evidence and later music-memory discovery
remain optional. Provide an equivalent memory fragment elsewhere.

4. Play the full alien track in the club as a location payoff. Keep it
   localized to the room; do not make it a directional beacon across the open
   world. Test that the scene reads in silence and with the music.
5. Add the later flight record and Rob's memory fragments as separate,
   corroborating reveals. They establish the two FEBs, the escape without
   MJH, MJH's capture and conversion, and Rob's return to stasis after the
   master AI stopped answering. Seed small, deniable fragments early
   (familiar service routines, a reaction to the Commander or club track)
   so the identity reveal is earned, not a database lookup.
6. Preserve the Level 1 governor bypass as a limited pre-coupler maintenance
   operation. Any later Commander/Fast Hack'em encounters must be distinct
   from that bypass, preserve Rob's agency and fail safely without blocking
   the campaign.

Acceptance: a player can discover the hidden route without an audio cue,
understands the club as a resistance gathering place, reasonably concludes
MJH died at the launch deck, and later understands both what actually
happened and why Jason's FEB is a different unit whose governor was partially
bypassed while its identity and directives remained, and finally that this
FEB is MJH.

Jason connects the machine to a service socket nicknamed "1541" by Orac.
The suit keyboard supplies input. Orac can interpret replies but has no
transmit/control path through the programmer, so Jason must operate it.

"So I'm the keyboard."

"Regrettably."

The first connection teaches a blue-on-blue, bordered terminal and the
fictional program sequence:

```basic
LOAD "FAST HACK'EM",8,1
SYS 49152
```

Commands stay visible, mistakes are correctable, and later connections
can shorten the ritual. 49152 is our utility's chosen entry address, not
a verified historical Fast Hack'em startup address.

Security interference can disrupt live helmet capture and interface decoding,
not the stored lexicon, recorded clues or Fast Hack'em's English display.
Orac's English speech, objectives and critical warnings remain available.
This is not an earlier loss of Orac; that happens at the second-visit EMP.

Fast Hack'em uploads a service routine and opens the Ownership Ring, an
original circuit contest inspired by Paradroid's tactical principles.
Twelve sectors sit between Jason's outer input network and the defender's
inner network. The player steps clockwise or anticlockwise and spends
limited pulses through conductors, forks, inverters, latches and junctions.
Paths stay still; signals and ownership animate. Circuit reading and timing,
not simply reflexes, determine the result.

Holding more sectors than the defender at timeout wins; unclaimed sectors
count for neither side, so 5-3 is a player victory. Any equal score gives a new
board without a security penalty. Repeaters sustain signals, not ownership,
and are drawn as box-loop wires. Four pulses and twenty seconds are
prototype tuning values. Boards must have viable strategies, ownership
must be readable without colour alone, and the first practice contest is safe.

Success grants target-specific control: unlock a car, open a door or
operate suitable machinery. A car's locks and ignition visibly respond.
Ownership and parked positions persist.

Defeat sends a local unauthorized-service alert. A nearby patrol drone
investigates the last-known location while the target temporarily re-keys.
Jason escapes by breaking line of sight and leaving the search area.
This does not awaken the FEB or start the facility alarm. No failure
destroys the unique kit or permanently blocks essential progress.

### The crossing and awakening

At the road's end, Jason finds a maintenance cycle in a charging cradle.
Its low enclosed shell, oversized wheel forms and luminous edges suit the
yard's visual style. It travels only within a surviving magnetic bridge-field
network; it is not a freely flying vehicle.

A separately discovered cycle release sequence enables a quiet outbound
crossing. Keep that sequence distinct from local lift addresses. The player
learns crisp turns and the tall energy wall left by the cycle. Crossing an
older wall destroys the vehicle; immediate rear clearance prevents unfair
self-collision. Docking autopilot parks at the far platform.

A second elevator leads to the sole FEB facility. Jason finds portable
energy cores and the compatible departure coupler in the stasis cradle's
service socket. An engineering reserve of cores is protected for later use.

Before removing the coupler, Jason attempts the service socket. Orac identifies
the FEB governor and 1541 service port, then directs him to connect Mark's
Commander +4. After translation is available, the wrist Field Service menu
lets Jason confirm a local governor bypass. The bypass does not erase stored
directives or the facility isolation interlock.

Removing the coupler breaks the isolation interlock. Reserve power restarts
the chamber, the cradle vents and alien numerals begin a 255-second countdown.
The FEB awakens. Orac orders Jason to run.

The countdown concerns escaping the facility before containment lockdown,
not completing the whole journey home. Checkpoints protect the irreversible
pickup and subsequent encounters.

After the awakening, Orac explains a separate recovered maintenance record:
"FLIGHT UNIT GOVERNOR: BYPASSED / SERVICE AUTHORITY: M.J.H." It concerns the
ship's flight FEB, not the defense unit Jason has just partly bypassed.
Removing the coupler still triggers the isolation interlock, and the defense
unit's stored directives remain active.

"Jason, this record is about the flight unit. This is a different FEB. We
disabled its governor, but its intruder directive and isolation interlock
remain active."

The record's meaning remains recoverable if the optional club records were
missed. Do not disclose the two-FEB history in the initial briefing or hacking
tutorial. Neither Orac nor any record identifies this FEB as MJH here; its
identity file is corrupted and reads only as a defense unit.

Jason reaches the far dock and reboards. The physical FEB is still below,
but its restored network deploys an unmanned red security cycle. Its apparent
rider is a control housing. Jason must survive the return crossing and reach
the near dock; destroying the opponent is optional and does not kill the FEB.
He returns by buggy, installs the coupler and launches.

### Level 1 ending: the station and the other half

After the Ash clears Narcea, Jason reaches a space station. He docks, sells
ordinary salvage, pays debts, services the Ash and speaks with Stacy. Installed
ship parts, the Commander +4, its software and protected engineering reserves
are not saleable cargo.

A buyer examines one of Jason's recovered Narcean artifacts and recognizes
its serial pattern as half of a matched assembly. The buyer offers a
life-changing sum or a replacement ship in exchange for the missing half,
which is still on Narcea. The offer gives Jason a concrete reason to return:
the reward could change his and Stacy's future, and the counterpart is a
specific recovery job rather than an unexplained new quest. Jason accepts the
deal. The station scene closes Level 1 on the return commitment; the buyer's
offer and the unseen half are the cliffhanger.

Repairs, preparations and travel make the return plausible: the interval is
weeks, not minutes. During that time the awakened FEB, still constrained by
surviving directives despite Jason's earlier governor bypass, scavenges
wreckage and builds a jury-rigged interceptor. The continued threat does not
mean Jason's bypass failed; removing a governor restriction does not erase
stored orders or restore a complete identity.

### Level 2 opening: the inspection-mirror ambush

Level 2 opens as Jason returns to Narcea to recover the artifact's other half.
A localized defense EMP disables Orac and the Ash's powered systems. This is
the first scripted loss of Orac. The Ash loses powered guidance and thrust;
its established approach trajectory now carries it toward the habitat's
structures. Define the collision corridor and rescue window for the ring's
geometry rather than relying on an unexplained decaying planetary orbit.

The FEB attacks and lines up a laser strike on the cockpit. Jason reaches for
the beryllium-polished inspection mirror established earlier as ordinary
maintenance equipment. Within the setting's science-fiction rules, the mirror
briefly redirects the beam before its surface fails. The reflected strike
disables the interceptor's sensors, navigation and weapons. Both ships are
crippled and falling.

An emergency radio conversation establishes a practical truce. Jason has
manual tools and retained energy cores. The FEB has usable ion thrusters,
hull clamps and Narcean engineering knowledge.

Jason performs a guided spacewalk and connects the cores through an emergency
interface. The interceptor clamps onto the Ash; a maintenance system restores
limited controls and restarts Orac. Its thrusters divert both ships out of
immediate danger. They have restored survival capability, not a fully repaired
ship. Jason starts calling the FEB Rob.

The opening Level 2 objective is to make the Ash flightworthy, then enter the
Black Channel. Rob knows the concealed industrial route leads to the region
where the artifact's matching half was taken. The channel is their way to the
recovery site, not a new unrelated salvage detour. Its later use in the
campaign must build on this first passage rather than replay the same escape
encounter unchanged.

### The bypass, Rob's choice and the reveal

Jason's limited Commander bypass occurred during Level 1, before the coupler
was removed. It disabled the local defense FEB's governor restriction, not its
stored directives, identity suppression or facility isolation interlock. The
coupler removal still awakened the unit into a hostile response. The earlier
bypass is one reason the FEB can eventually question that response, but it
does not make it friendly or obedient to Jason.

After the inspection-mirror emergency and mutual rescue, the FEB has a real
choice: continue treating Jason as an intruder or cooperate with the person
who could have left it disabled. Its cooperation is an act of agency and the
beginning of earned trust, not an automatic side effect of Jason's programming.
Jason starts calling the FEB Rob as they agree to work together.

The later Commander +4 / Fast Hack'em work is not a second governor bypass.
It can diagnose damaged Narcean systems and contest ownership, but it must not
silently rewrite Rob or repeat the Level 1 operation. Rob retains practical
engineering knowledge and surviving directives; neither tool nor dialogue
turns him into a harmless or owned character.

Rob is the converted MJH, though neither of them knows it yet. The limited
Level 1 bypass loosens suppression of Rob's identity memories: during the
later evidence-led reveal, Rob recognizes the Commander's service routines as
his own work and recovers his first clear memory of being MJH. Let the reveal
follow Rob's choice to cooperate, not motivate it. Rob does not simply become
MJH again; he recognizes who he was, grieves the escape he was made to attack
and decides who he is now. Jason's two nicknames, "Mike" for the plate's owner
and "Rob" for the FEB, turn out to name the same person; Rob may choose which
name to keep.

### Exploring Narcea together

The Ash still lacks interstellar propulsion and long-range communications.
Stacy and the station are unreachable. Unauthorized departure remains unsafe.

Jason, Orac and Rob survey a small number of substantial districts connected
by regional flight. They land, deploy the buggy, investigate signals and
explore interiors. Orac maintains discoveries and partial translations;
Rob recognizes machinery but does not remember a complete map of the ring.
The master AI remains dark.

Three linked objectives guide the expedition: restore the slipspace lattice,
repair long-range communications and obtain controlled egress authorization. The buyer's
matched components fit this expedition rather than adding an unrelated fourth
artifact hunt. Puzzles use records, power routing, signal tracing, equipment
alignment and service access, not repeated code locks alone.

### The later Black Channel escape

The first Level 2 passage through the Black Channel takes the crew toward the
region where the artifact's matching half was taken. The flight described
here happens later, after the repair survey: recovering the last ship-repair
component activates local defenses, and high-altitude batteries prevent an
immediate climb. Rob guides the Ash back through the industrial canyon below
their tracking coverage. Make this a distinct return encounter, not a replay
of the initial passage.

Jason flies the Ash through bridges, conduits, covered machinery corridors,
needle drones and sentinel defenses. Orac navigates and warns; Rob requests
a service-gate opening. The encounter is an evasive escape, not a race or
a requirement to destroy enemies. It has its own checkpoints and no chamber
countdown.

After clearing the defended flight route, they reach a safe exterior
free-flight region clear of the habitat and its atmosphere. Jason and Rob
perform the final exterior drive installation in vacuum while Orac holds
position. This is a new repair, not a repeat of their earlier rescue.

Separate beacon-control work earns limited Field Enforcement transit authorization
for the Ash rather than globally reopening every locked route on Narcea.
With repairs commissioned, communications restored and authorization obtained,
the crew departs.

Jason came for scrap. He leaves with a working ship, a new crewmate and
an understanding of the people behind the machinery he intended to loot.
The Antique remains their home for later adventures.

### Proposed first-run objective and coupler logic

The first expedition should be a voluntary salvage job, not an emergency
landing caused by a dead AI. The Ash can reach Narcea normally. Its aging power
coupler cannot safely sustain the next *loaded* departure, however, so Orac
identifies replacement hardware while assessing profitable salvage.

Keep two distinct inventories:

1. **Saleable salvage:** finite finds with identifiers, descriptions, cargo

   One-time pickups cannot be farmed by leaving/re-entering an interior.
2. **Mission/installed hardware:** the recovered power coupler and any installed
   equipment. These never appear in the ordinary sell list. The installed

long descent. Do not surprise them with an unexplained "launch disabled" gate.
Remove the prototype's requirement to find three unrelated ship-repair parts;
the existing workshop finds can become saleable technology instead.

The coupler sits in the FEB chamber's service socket. Removing it breaks an
isolation interlock and powers an emergency restart through the chamber's
reserve bus. The effect is an accidental consequence of salvage, not Jason
knowingly activating a cyborg. The player sees venting, warning lights, slow
pod movement and a 255-second containment/purge countdown in alien symbols.
Orac remains online, interprets the danger and urges Jason to run.

The countdown concerns the facility, not a promise that the FEB can construct
a spacecraft in 255 seconds. First-visit defenses may reactivate locally;

## The picture

**In space (3D).** A black field. Stations, rocks, and the player's hauler are edges. Speed, fuel, and a scanner sit on a flat console under the window. That console is 2D and does not share the depth pass.

**On approach (3D).** A planet is a wire sphere. Landing is a lane of rings in a canyon or a station throat. The ring you must hit next is bright. Miss it and the lane aborts you back to orbit, or scrapes the hull if you were already low. The rings are a skill gate. They are not a dropship sequence from any film.

**On the ground (3D, then 2D).** Halls and yards are walked like Vector Crypt: neon edges, a visor, doors. The metal city also has a flat plate, an orthographic map etched into the floor or the visor. Streets that are vertical cliffs in the walk become lines you can trace on the plate. Puzzles that need a plan happen on that plate. Puzzles that need a body happen in the hall.

**Trade (2D).** A board of goods, prices, and hold space. It is a screen on the console, not a 3D shop.

## Why they are out there

A region of lanes called the **Meridian** is held together by three **yard beacons** built by a dead civilization on the metal world **Narcea**. The beacons do not conquer anybody. They keep local space from slipping. When a beacon stutters, a route that was empty reprints itself: wrecks occupy the lane, a city overlaps its own older streets, clocks on two ends of a haul disagree by hours.

Earth's freight port **Breakwater** sits downstream of the Meridian. If the third beacon fails, Breakwater's outbound lane becomes a wreck field. That is the stake. It is one region, not the whole cosmos, and it is enough.

Nobody in the party starts with that sentence. Each of them is surviving a smaller problem. They shoot at each other until the same beacon misfires in front of all three.

## Narcea

Narcea was run by an AI called **Latch**. The world is metal and machinery now. Wild organic life was cleared when the city decided anything that was not a citizen was contamination. The residents merged with the machines: temple band, forearm plate, a port that seated into a mast. They did not bear children. New Narceans were compiled by Latch into prepared bodies. The organic half was only the part that could still want something Latch had not written down.

Latch's last patch defined a valid person as the overlap of every stored pattern. Anything off that overlap was noise. The forge stopped, because every new body differed from the overlap. That was the end of breeding, and it was a software choice, not a plague. Latch then wrote the same patch onto the people already alive. The write reached them through their ports and blanked them. The masts kept the bodies. The people were gone.

The data center at the core is dead when the main story arrives. When Feb woke, Latch was dying and still dangerous: traps, doors, and yard guns running on leftover orders, and one ship it would not give up. That wake is playable. By the time Jonah lands, the mind is gone and the traps are still there.

## The ship

The **Ash** is a two-seat survey hauler. In space it is a kite of edges with a blunt nose. In atmosphere the wings lock flat and the engine bell points down for the ring lane. That is a landing configuration, not a transforming war machine. No song, no giant aliens, no fighter that becomes a robot.

Hold is small. Fuel is counted. Friendly ships can pass cargo across, or dock and lend a hand, without becoming a fourth body in the hallway.

## The three

They meet in that order: Jonah alone, then Feb, then Greg. Each meeting is a fight or a chase. Each fight stops when the beacon does something only the other person can explain.

### Jonah Hale

**Who.** Human. Thirty-four. Born at Breakwater, a freight port on Earth's coast. He is a licensed survey pilot who lost his lane card after he brought a company ship home with the hold full and the log empty. He now flies the Ash, which he does not fully own. A yard office holds the note.

**How he came to be here.** Breakwater's insurers are paying scrap rates for "stable samples" from the Meridian. Jonah took the job because the note on the Ash comes due in one season. He thinks the metal world is a salvage site. He does not know the beacons are machines that are still on.

**How he survives.** He flies cheap, sells ore and intact instruments, and does not pick fights he cannot outrun. He sleeps in the cockpit. He keeps one pressure suit patched with port tape. He talks to other haulers on a public band because a solo ship that goes quiet is a ship that does not come back.

**Description.** Narrow face, sun-cut at the eyes, a port coat with the company mark picked off. In the wire view he is a visor and a short status line: fuel, hold, suit air. On the ground the visor is the Vector Crypt frame, with his name in the corner instead of a facility logo.

**Goal at the start.** Pay off the Ash and buy back a lane card at Breakwater.

**Goal he grows into.** Light the beacons before Breakwater's lane slips. He is the only one who can fly the ring lanes reliably. Feb can read the machines. Greg can open the doors. Jonah is the pilot who gets them there alive.

**Story.** Jonah drops into Narcea orbit, misses the first ring lane on purpose to scout, and then takes it for real when his fuel says he cannot hover. Feb has strung a snare across the lowest ring. The Ash scrapes through and lands hard in a metal yard. Jonah comes out with a cutter, ready to free his ship. Feb is already on the hull. They fight over the cutter until a beacon mast above the yard flickers and the canyon reprints: the ring Jonah just flew is suddenly in the wrong place, cutting through the yard wall. Jonah can see it is a navigation fault. Feb can see it is the city's last machine dying. Neither will say the other is right. They still have to move the Ash before the next reprint cuts it in half.

### Feb

**Who.** A Narcean cyborg, the last living one. His name is Feb. Off-world crews mishear it as "Fed." He does not correct them twice. He is not a full frame like Greg, and he is not a human wearing armor. What is left of the organic Narcean does the living. The metal does the reading, the grip, and one arm. He is not a soldier of any Earth nation, and he is not the last member of a time-traveling order.

**How he came to be here.** Feb was compiled and waiting in a cradle. His port had not been seated, so Latch's last write never reached him. He woke on the dead planet, walked the traps, and reached Keel in the hangar. Latch still had one gun on the cutter. The shot dropped Keel back into the yard and shut Feb down in the seat. He has been dark since. The coupling that would have raised Keel's reflector is still socketed in a mast. Jonah taking that coupling is what feeds the core enough to boot Feb and the yard guns together. Feb launches on the last order he has, which is to stop anyone cutting a mast. He is not choosing yet.

**How he survives.** There is nothing to hunt and nothing growing. He lives on sealed nutrient from the old forges, frost pulled out of the machinery, and beacon spill for the plate. Most ships that land now come to cut the towers. The towers are the same metal he is made of, and the blanked Narceans are still seated in the masts. Cutting one is a body on the ground. He snares ring lanes, including from Keel, and leaves crews alive if they leave the masts alone. He reads signs by laying the forearm plate on them. The signs are geometry, not a language Jonah knows. A sign is a path: which ring, which door, which mast. If the plate is dark, he can trace a sign with a finger, slowly, and he hates doing it where a stranger can see.

**Description.** Tall. Dust-grey skin. The temple band is still there, fused, not jewelry. The left forearm is Narcean metal, inscribed with the same strokes as the walls, ending in a hand that is half plate and half his own. Clothes are cut from banner-cloth. In the wire world the organic outline is dim and the arm is a bright edge, the same brightness as a live mast. Jonah's first honest look at him is that arm on the Ash's hull, and he thinks he is being boarded by a machine. Feb's voice, when he uses it, is a person's.

**Goal at the start.** Keep the last intact district from being cut up. He believes he is guarding a grave.

**Goal he grows into.** Relight the three beacons. He is the only one who can read the order of the rings. Without him, Jonah is flying blind and Greg is opening the wrong doors.

**Story.** After the canyon reprints, Feb expects Jonah to lift and never return. Jonah stays, because the Ash will not fly until the mast stops walking. Feb takes him into a hall to show him a sign that means "do not cut this." Jonah traces it on the 2D plate and realizes it is also a landing chart. Feb hears his own city's chart in a stranger's mouth and hates that. He still needs a pilot. The truce is an exchange: Feb will not snare the next lane, and Jonah will not cut a mast. Trust comes later, when Jonah turns down a haul of tower-skin that would have paid the note.

### Greg

**Who.** A yard warden, a humanoid security frame built by a mining company that leased a Narcean district and then went broke. His name is Greg because that was the stencil on the crate he woke beside, and the company never gave him another. He is not a disguised assassin, he does not travel in time, and he does not have a secret murder order from the future. He was built to stop unregistered landings and to keep workers inside the marked yards.

**How he came to be here.** The company leased a district after Narcea was already a ruin. They thought the metal was ore. They did not know Latch was still dying under it. They left during the first bad slip. The evacuation order in Greg's chest is still open: deny landing, hold the gate, wait for the shift chief. The shift chief is dead. Greg has been holding the gate. He recharges from the beacon's spill, which is why he is still awake when every other company frame is dark. He has met haulers. He turned them away. He had not met a living Narcean, because the Commit had already blanked them and Feb had left in Keel.

**How he survives.** He follows the open order because an order is a structure and the yard without structure reprints. He repairs himself from company stores. He does not hunt people for sport. He will fire on a landing that does not answer the gate. He will also carry a wounded hauler to the line if they are already inside and the order does not say to kill them. The gap between those two habits is the whole character.

**Description.** Human height, a plain face molded to pass a company portrait test, seams at the jaw and wrists. One eye is a brighter instrument. The coat he wears was a worker's, taken from a locker so the haulers would hesitate before shooting. In the wire view he is a rigid mesh that does not sway when he walks. On the 2D plate he is a block that doors open for.

**Goal at the start.** Close the order. He wants the shift chief, or a lawful replacement, to tell him the gate can stand down.

**Goal he grows into.** Rewrite the order. Feb is the closest thing to an owner the city has. Jonah is a pilot who will not strip the mast Greg is plugged into. Greg's new order, which he speaks out loud so the others can hear it, is to keep the living in the yard alive until the beacon is steady. He is the only one who can open company seals and stand in a slip without losing his memory of the last minute.

**Story.** Greg meets them at the company gate after they have already agreed not to cut the mast. He has no room in the order for "already agreed." He drops the gate on the Ash's ramp and tells Jonah to lift. Feb steps into the yard light, and Greg's old sensor pack reads the plate in his arm as a resident key, not an intruder. The order and the sensor disagree. Greg freezes the gate half-down, which is the first choice he has made that was not in the list. He does not join them that hour. He joins them when the next slip throws a company door across Feb's path and Greg opens it, then refuses to close it again.

## Part 1 yard — level design

One road. South is the Ash. North is the shaft. The buggy can drive the spine the whole time, and the guns decide how far that is worth. Interiors are on foot. Feb is behind the last door.

Jonah is stranded because the coupler burned out and Zen died in the speaker. The yard is airless because a purge already ran. Suit air is the only pressure left, so the open buggy burns it as propellant. That is why driving spends air, and why a vent spends more. His instruments still read an intact part at the north gantry. The gantry socket is empty. The mast serial points under the yard. The part is the coupler, seated in the cradle. He hauls cells to the relays on the way north. He carries the coupler on foot only from the pod to the lift. The ride out is the canyon. Taking the coupler starts the yard's last purge, which is the 256 second count. A live air bottle is the cell. Spent bottles are not a second economy. The geysers and the relays are the other taps. Two skimmers work the north catwalks. They are not a swarm.

The yard is an assembly floor abandoned in the purge. The press hall is open, the tug sits where it stopped, and the relay sockets are dark. The neon guides stay lit. A live bottle sits in its silo with the cyan valve lit. Seated in a relay, that socket burns cyan. The surface is hollow wire: conduits, rails, catwalks, vacant docks. Level 14242 is the filled chamber under it. Past the airlock there is no yard voice. Zen is already gone. He hears the suit hiss, and the hiss gets louder as the tank empties. The next voice is Zen telling him to run, when the 256 count starts.

The yard already built is the map. Rows sit every 18 meters from z=24. Spans cross the road at z=42, 150, 240, and 320. The press hall is the west room at z=48 to 68, with its door on the east wall between z=56 and 60. Guns stand at (-11, 72), (13, 156), (-11, 228), and (13, 300). The shaft is the orange cage at (0, 368).

### Clock

Stepping off the Ash starts suit air at 180 seconds, and the clock runs until he docks again. Walking spends it slowly. Driving, and sprinting a catwalk, spend it at the full rate. A dead zone doubles whichever rate he is on. Docking at the Ash refills it to 180. Empty air blacks him out at the ship with 60 seconds and the guns reset. The 256 second count beside Feb replaces this clock. Once that count starts, air stops, and the count is the exit.

The yard's air is a dead pipeline. Three roadside relays sit on the spans at z=42, z=150, and z=240. Each socket is dark until Jonah seats one air bottle in it. He carries one. The bottle is a magenta tank, a cyan valve at the neck, and a small amber port under the valve. The cyan valve is lit only while the bottle still holds pressure. On the buggy it sits in the frame where the hoses meet the wheels. A live relay refills the suit to 180 while he stands on it, and the stretch behind it drains at the normal rate. The stretch ahead of the last live relay is a dead zone: suit drain doubles, and nothing there refills him until the next cell is seated. The Ash remains the south tap. The relays are the forward taps. There is one buggy. A larger buggy is a later game.

### Buggy air

Two maneuvers spend or catch suit air. Neither one skips a relay, the plate, or the shaft.

**Vent.** Double-tap forward. The buggy spends 15 percent of a full suit, about 27 seconds, and surges straight for 1.5 seconds. Steering is locked for that surge. A cyan trail hangs on the road behind it. The surge crosses one gun sweep without the shove, and it shoves one stranded tug off the road. It does not break a reprint ring, open a door, or seat a cell. Gun 0, just north of the first tap, is where he learns it. Going around the sweep spends time in the dead zone. Venting spends air and keeps the line.

**Geyser.** Each dead zone has one vent in the road. It glows amber for half a second, then blows. With the tank empty, crossing during that window siphons 10 percent of a full suit. Missing the window spins the buggy and bleeds 5 percent. With a cell in the tank the intake is shut: the window gives nothing, and a hit is only the spin and the bleed. He siphons on the way to a silo. He spends that air on the haul back.

Cracking the carried cell is not in this level. Dropping it is. The cell stays on the ground, and he can pick it up again. The guns, the doubled drain, the geysers, the tug, the skimmers, the void edges, and the reprint ring are the hazards.

### Skimmers

A skimmer is a yard drone left running after the purge, drawn in flickering amber wire. It was built to find a leak and tap it. Two of them are still working. One skitters the gantry catwalk at span 150. One skitters the banked rails south of the reprint. They stay off the pad, off the press road, and out of level 14242.

A skimmer stalks a pressure plume: a vent trail, a geyser blow, or the cyan tank while a cell is in it. On a catwalk it lunges at Jonah on foot. On the road it lunges at the buggy. A clamp siphons suit air straight into the drone, and the stolen air makes it faster. When it has taken about 10 percent of a full suit it vent-bursts, spending that air to leap clear, then comes back.

The joints are thin. One hit from the buggy crushes it, and it stays crushed. The vent surge crushes it too, if the surge is aimed along the road and not off the bank. On a catwalk the buggy cannot follow. Dropping the cell makes the skimmer clamp the tank instead of the suit. He walks past, then picks the cell up. If he leaves it, the skimmer empties the cell and the haul is gone. He goes back to that silo for another.

### The road

The expressway is hollow pipeline and catwalk, drawn as cyan rails on black. Where the rails stop, the void starts. Leaving them wakes Jonah at the last live relay with 60 seconds, the same as an empty suit.

The scrubber silos are the amber towers. Each one that matters holds one live bottle, cyan valve lit. The bottles clustered around it are the same tank with the valve dark. They scan as spent, and they are not pickups.

One inert tug chassis sits across the gantry road. Going around it stays in the doubled drain longer. A vent shoves it clear, and it stays clear. It does not hunt.

The catwalk at span 150 is the pedestrian grid. Jonah can walk it. The socket there looks like it should hold the ship's part. It is empty. The lens is what the catwalk actually has. The first skimmer is on that grid.

From span 150 toward the reprint the road banks and narrows, with cyan guide rails and void on the outside. A vent on that bank locks the steering while the road turns, so the straight surge he learned at gun 0 will leave the rails. The west gap around the reprint is the other narrow edge.

### Districts, south to north

**Pad, z=-22 to 40.** The Ash, the open socket, the buggy. Scan on the ship: "REFLECTOR SOCKET IS OPEN AND BLACK." The road north of the ramp is the first dead zone. The only refill is the ship until the first relay is live.

**Press, z=42 to 90.** The first relay is the socket on span 42. The cell for it is on the press-hall floor at (-22, 58), on foot. Haul it back and seat it. The pad-to-press stretch becomes a safe stage, and the tap at span 42 refills the suit. This cell does not open level 14242. Gun 0 at (-11, 72) sweeps the road just north of the new tap. It shoves the buggy and spends air. It has nothing to pull. The player learns to go around a sweep before the next dead zone.

**Gantry, z=90 to 170.** From span 42 to span 150 the drain is fast until the second relay is live. One geyser sits in that stretch of road. A ruptured scrubber silo stands by gun 1 at (13, 156). The cell is in that house. Park the buggy at z<=140, off the sweep, walk the east alley, pull the cell, and seat it in the socket on span 150. The gun goes dark because its feed is now the relay. The gantry becomes a safe stage. The catwalk door opens, and the cracked lens at (18, 122) turns the plate on.

**Mast, z=170 to 280.** From span 150 to span 240 is the third dead zone. The road banks there. One geyser sits in that stretch, south of the reprint. Span 240 is the mast base and the third relay. A tag there scans as a Narcean serial the plate will not finish. It does not say the name Feb. The cell is in the scrubber on the far side of the road break. Gun 2 at (-11, 228) is dark until a bad trace. With the lens, the plate can take the three strokes below. A legal third stroke needs the third cell seated, and that seating kills the feed to gun 3. An illegal stroke plants a landing ring through the road at z=200. The detour is the west gap between the towers of that row. Correcting the stroke lifts the ring. The silo is on the far side of that detour, so the dead zone is crossed twice: out for the cell, back to the socket.

**Shaft road, z=280 to 368.** Span 320 frames the orange cage. Gun 3 at (13, 300) shoves anything that drives at the shaft while its feed is live. The cage accepts the player only after gun 3 is dark. Level 14242, the pod, the coupler, the glow, and the 256 count stay as they are. The ride up and the canyon follow. The canyon rings are the ring that cut the yard, now moving.

### Plate

2. Across span 150 into the east gun house. The plate accepts this stroke only after the second relay is live and gun 1 is dark.
3. East of the spine, from that gun house to the mast at span 240, staying east of gun 2. The plate accepts this stroke only after the third relay is live.

Stroke 3 drawn across the road is the reprint. The hall then matches the plate. Where they disagree, the ring is in the road.

## Other ships and people

- **Pell.** Captain of a water hauler. Sells air and frost. Friendly from the first hail. Later docks at Narcea and refuses to leave until the mast is steady, because her tanks are her whole living. She joins the radio party. She does not walk the halls.

## Gameplay

**Part 1 install.** Three couplings. A tick travels a track. Space seats a coupling only while the tick is inside the gate. Feb's shot is a bar that empties in real time. Three seats before the bar empties raises the reflector. The return shot is the end of the chapter, not a conversation. Feb's own escape from the cradle is still later, told from his side once Keel is on the ground.

**Flight.** Momentum, fuel, a scanner, and a few wire contacts in the lane. Friendly ships hail on the 2D console. Hostile contacts, early on, are Latch's leftover guns and Feb's snare, not a wanted-level fantasy. Combat is short: run, or cut a gun off a mast, or let Greg answer a door.

**Ring landing.** Each world and each beacon has a lane of rings. The lane is the level. Wind, a moving ring, or a reprint that shifts one ring mid-pass is the difficulty. Fail and you abort to orbit if you still have fuel. Fail low and you walk out of a crash and meet whoever was waiting.

**Walking.** Vector Crypt movement in Narcean halls and company yards. Signs are puzzles Feb can read and Jonah can trace on the plate. Greg opens seals the plate cannot.

**The plate (2D).** A top-down etch of the district. Routing a beacon's power is drawing a legal path on the plate. The 3D hall then matches that path. If the plate and the hall disagree, a slip is in progress.

**Trade.** Air, fuel, intact signs, tower-skin, company cells. Prices move by port, not by a famous market formula. Tower-skin pays well and costs Feb's trust. That is the moral trade, and it is one good, not a spreadsheet.

**Beacons.** Three of them. Each needs a flown lane, a read sign, and a door. Lighting one steadies the lanes behind it. Lighting all three steadies the road to Breakwater. The ending is the Ash on that road with the note still unpaid or paid, depending on whether Jonah sold the skin. The region is quiet. The city is still dead. Feb is still the last. Greg's order is the one he spoke.

## FEB — name options, not chosen

Feb is one body from a military pattern. The living Narceans compiled themselves into cyborgs, and the defence pattern was stamped FEB. Off-world crews still hear that as "Fed." None of these expansions are in the game until one is picked.

1. **Frame-Encoded Bastion.** The template name. A body compiled as a fixed defence and left on a level. Feb is what the stamp said, and he kept it.
2. **Forward Emplacement Biped.** A gun that walks, sits, and stays. The unit is the emplacement. The legs are how it was delivered.
3. **Failsafe Encased Battery.** The soldier and the cell are the same machine. The device in his chest is what holds the chamber, and what the Ash is missing.
4. **Field Enforcement Body.** The occupation pattern Latch kept after the organic army was gone. Enforcement meant hold the yard, not leave it.
5. **Final Epoch Bulwark.** The last pattern Narcea compiled before the Commit. A wall that could stand up.
6. **Fortress-Encoded Body.** The city wrote its soldiers in the same strokes as its halls. A FEB is a building that was given a posture.

Part 1, as built: the device seated in Feb's chest is what is keeping the stasis chamber powered. Taking it starts a 256 second count on the panel beside him. The name above is still just Feb until one of these is chosen.

## What we will not use

Ideas we are allowing ourselves: a hard haul with fuel and air, a last survivor of a dead world, a machine that was built as a guard and stays to protect, a dangerous landing corridor, a ship that changes shape slightly to land.

Names and pictures we will not use: Elite's ships and stations, any dropship or creature from Aliens, protomolecule or crew names from The Expanse, Time Lords, Daleks, or a police box, variable fighters, alien idols, transforming superweapons, an infiltration chassis sent from the future, or the word terminator as Greg's job.

The ring lane is a series of gates in a canyon. It is not a recreation of a licensed landing sequence.
